For early humans, time was simple. The sun came up — you got up. The sun went down — you went to sleep.

Seasons mattered way more than minutes. When will the river flood? When do the animals move? When should we plant?

So people watched the sky. Monuments like Stonehenge line up with the sun on the solstices — basically, giant stone calendars.

But to track time within a single day, you needed something simpler: a stick. Plant it in the ground, and its shadow moves as the sun crosses the sky.

The ancient Egyptians turned this idea into real tools. Around three and a half thousand years ago, they were using shadow clocks and sundials to split daylight into parts.

They divided the day into twelve hours, and the night into another twelve. That's where our twenty-four hour day comes from.

But here's the twist: those hours weren't equal. Daylight was always split into twelve parts, so a summer hour was longer than a winter hour.

Imagine your work shift getting longer every single summer. No thanks.

And sundials had one huge bug. They don't work at night. Or when it's cloudy.
