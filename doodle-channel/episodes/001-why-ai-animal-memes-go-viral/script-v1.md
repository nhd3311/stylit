## COLD OPEN — The Bunnies (0:00)

July 2025. A grainy night-vision clip lands on TikTok: a backyard trampoline, and a bunch of wild bunnies bouncing on it, one after another. [1]
▸ ⭐ Grainy cobalt "security cam" frame with a timestamp shape (no real digits), cut-paper bunnies bouncing on a trampoline; Pip watching on a phone, delighted.

It looks like home security footage. It racks up hundreds of millions of views. People share it with their parents. [1] ⚠️
▸ Pip forwarding the clip to a whole family tree of little phones.

Then someone notices: one of the bunnies just… vanishes. Mid-jump. [1]
▸ Freeze-frame, a vermilion circle around an empty spot where a bunny was.

The whole thing was made by AI.
▸ Pip's jaw drops; the phone screen glitches into halftone dots.

And that's not even the weirdest part. Scroll a little further, and you'll find sad AI cats crying in the rain to a meowing song, sharks wearing sneakers with Italian-sounding names, and fruit acting out reality shows. [2][3]
▸ Fast collage montage of OUR invented creatures: a crying orange cat under a cut-paper umbrella, a fish with sneakers, a croissant with legs.

A lot of these clips are barely videos at all. Just a picture of an animal, a catchy sound, and ten seconds of your life.
▸ Minimal frame: one animal picture + a music-note shape + a tiny stopwatch.

So why do they work so well? Why does your thumb stop? The answer starts about seventeen thousand years ago.
▸ Pip's "?" curl glows; the phone slowly turns into a cave wall. TITLE CARD.

## CH1 — We've Always Done This (≈0:57)

In the caves of Lascaux, in France, people painted horses, deer and wild bulls on the walls, around seventeen thousand years ago. Long before writing, humans were already sharing pictures of animals. [4]
▸ ⭐ Caveman-Pip with a torch in front of a cave wall of mustard and vermilion animals.

The ancient Egyptians went further. They were so devoted to cats that they mummified them in huge numbers, as offerings to the cat goddess Bastet. [5]
▸ Egyptian-Pip bowing to a giant cat statue with earrings.

In medieval Europe, monks copying holy books by hand got bored, and started doodling in the margins. One very popular doodle? Knights in full armour… fighting snails. Historians still aren't sure why. [6]
▸ ⭐ An old manuscript page; in the margin, a tiny knight charging at a snail. Monk-Pip giggling with a quill.

In the 1870s, a photographer in Brighton, England, named Harry Pointer posed his cats drinking tea, riding tricycles and roller-skating, and sold the photos with funny captions. Over a hundred and thirty years before the internet's LOLcats. [7]
▸ Victorian-Pip behind a big box camera; a cat in a tiny teacup pose; caption space left empty (added in edit).

Then came the internet, and animals took over. LOLcats in the 2000s. Grumpy Cat. Doge. Every few years, a new animal becomes the face of online culture. [8]
▸ A timeline of chunky old monitors, each showing one of OUR generic cat/dog doodles.

So AI didn't invent the animal meme. It just removed the last limit: reality.
▸ Pip snipping a rope labelled with an empty sign; a flood of impossible creatures pours in.

## CH2 — The Cute Button (≈2:03)

Let's start with the biology. In 1943, the scientist Konrad Lorenz described something he called the "baby schema": big eyes, a round face, a big head, a small nose and mouth. [9]
▸ Diagram: Pip's own face with arrows: BIG EYES, ROUND HEAD (labels added in edit). Pip looks proud.

Our brains are wired to react to those features, because they help us care for babies. And research suggests the brain reacts to an infant face in about a seventh of a second, before you've even consciously thought about it. [9] ⚠️
▸ A brain shape with a mustard lightning bolt; a stopwatch at 1/7.

Here's the trick: it doesn't only work on babies. Puppies, kittens, cartoon characters… and AI animals can push those features as far as they want. Bigger eyes. Rounder heads. Softer fur.
▸ A cat getting rounder and rounder in four steps until it's a perfect ball with huge eyes.

Researchers in Norway even found that cute animal videos can trigger a specific emotion they call "kama muta": that warm, chest-swelling, "aww" feeling of being moved. [10]
▸ Pip holding his chest, eyes sparkling, little hearts floating.

So every cute AI animal is basically pressing a button in your head that evolution installed a very long time ago.
▸ A giant vermilion button labelled with a heart, and a mitten hand pressing it.

## CH3 — Wrong, But Safe (≈3:03)

But cute alone doesn't explain a shark in sneakers.
▸ Our invented fish-with-sneakers walking down a street; Pip staring.

For that, we need a theory of comedy. Psychologists Peter McGraw and Caleb Warren call it the "benign violation theory": we laugh when something is wrong… but also harmless. [11]
▸ Split frame: left a vermilion "WRONG" zone, right a cobalt "SAFE" zone, and the sneaker fish standing exactly on the line.

A cat in a business suit? Wrong. But nobody's hurt. Funny.
A crocodile that's also an airplane? Very wrong. Totally harmless. Even funnier.
▸ Quick gags: cat in a suit at a tiny desk; a crocodile-shaped plane flying past.

And AI is the perfect machine for this. It can mash any two things together in seconds, at zero cost. Things that would take an animator days to draw.
▸ A cut-paper "blender" machine: an animal goes in one side, an object in the other, a hybrid pops out.

## CH4 — The Song Does Half the Work (≈3:39)

Now turn the sound on. Because on TikTok, the music isn't decoration. It's half the meme.
▸ Pip putting on giant headphones; sound-wave shapes fill the frame.

One of the earliest AI animal trends, back in 2024, was simply sad pictures of an orange cat, set to a meowed version of a hit pop ballad. Toddlers watching it burst into tears. And videos of those crying toddlers went viral too. [2]
▸ A sad orange cat (ours) under rain; a tiny toddler-Pip with streaming tears; a phone filming the toddler.

Why does the song matter so much? Music researchers studying "earworms" found that the biggest predictor of a song getting stuck in your head is simply having heard it recently. [12]
▸ A worm wearing headphones crawling into an ear-shaped cave.

And on TikTok, a sound is reused across thousands of videos. Every new clip with the same sound is another repetition. Another hook in your memory. TikTok even says its recommendations take into account a video's sounds, captions and hashtags. [13]
▸ ⭐ One music-note shape splitting into hundreds of tiny phone screens, all pulsing to the same beat.

Plus, a picture and a song need zero translation. A clip made in Indonesia works in Brazil, Vietnam, or the US. No subtitles, no accents, no problem.
▸ Globe with phones popping up on every continent, all showing the same creature.

## CH5 — Built for the Scroll (≈4:42)

Then there's length. Many of these clips are under fifteen seconds. They're over before you've decided whether to skip.
▸ Pip's thumb hovering over a phone; the clip ends before the thumb lands.

And TikTok has said that watching a video all the way through is one of the signals it uses to decide what you'll like. Short clips get finished. Short clips loop. And a loop looks like interest. [13] ⚠️
▸ A looping arrow made of paper strips circling a phone.

Some aren't even videos. They're photo slideshows you swipe through yourself: and every swipe is an action the app can count. [14]
▸ Pip swiping through a stack of animal pictures like playing cards.

Put it together, and you get the perfect formula: a cute button, a funny violation, a catchy sound, and a length your thumb can't beat.
▸ A recipe card: four icons (heart button, sneaker fish, music note, stopwatch) going into a cooking pot.

## CH6 — The Audience Writes the Story (≈5:22)

But the formula only explains the click. The craze comes from you.
▸ Pip pointing straight at the viewer.

Take "Italian brainrot", which exploded in 2025: absurd AI creatures with fake Italian names, like Tralalero Tralala. The names are nonsense, but they're incredibly fun to say out loud. [3] ⚠️
▸ Kids-Pips shouting a name; the letters (added in edit) bounce like rubber.

And they're not all Italian. One of the most famous, "Tung Tung Tung Sahur", comes from Indonesia: its name copies the sound of drums beaten to wake people for the pre-dawn meal during Ramadan. [3]
▸ A wooden drum beaten at night; sound waves over rooftops (no character recreation).

Kids started inventing backstories, rivalries, and power rankings for these characters, collecting them like trading cards. The audience wrote the lore.
▸ A wall of trading cards with OUR invented creatures, kids-Pips pinning red strings between them.

And in October 2025, a Roblox game built around these creatures, "Steal a Brainrot", hit more than twenty-five million players at the same time: a record for a single Roblox game. [15]
▸ ⭐ A giant crowd of tiny avatar-Pips filling a cut-paper stadium.

A meme that started as a ten-second clip had turned into a whole universe.
▸ The universe zooms out: a single phone in the middle of a galaxy of creatures.

## CH7 — The Factory (≈6:19)

Of course, someone is making all of this. And a lot of it isn't made for fun.
▸ Behind a curtain: a cut-paper factory with conveyor belts of creatures.

Researchers who interviewed AI video creators in China found they treat virality like engineering: study what already went viral, turn it into a repeatable format, test it, then scale it across multiple accounts. [16]
▸ Creator-Pip at a desk with six phones, sticky notes, graphs going up.

Some accounts post dozens of AI animal clips every single day. [17]
▸ A tower of phones, each pumping out creatures like a vending machine.

The money mostly comes from platform payout programs: more views, more money. A few creators make thousands of dollars a month. Most make very little. [16][18] ⚠️
▸ A few creators on top of a coin mountain; a big crowd at the bottom holding single coins.

When making a video costs almost nothing, the smart move is to make a lot of them. And that's exactly what happened.
▸ The factory overflowing, creatures spilling out of the windows.

## CH8 — The Backlash (≈7:02)

Not everyone is laughing.
▸ Pip's smile fades; the colours dim to cobalt.

"Brain rot" was Oxford's Word of the Year for 2024, defined as the supposed deterioration of your mind from consuming too much trivial online content. The term goes back to Henry David Thoreau, in 1854. [19]
▸ A Thoreau-like figure at a lakeside cabin writing with a quill; Pip peeking.

In a 2026 survey, more than half of social media users said they see "AI slop" often or very often. [20]
▸ Pip buried in a pile of identical creatures, only his "?" curl sticking out.

The platforms are reacting. TikTok added a setting that lets you see more or less AI-generated content in your feed. And in July 2026, YouTube tightened its rules on mass-produced, repetitive AI videos, cutting them off from ad money. [21][22]
▸ A big slider (cut-paper) being pulled down; a YouTube-like red button with a padlock (generic, no logo).

Some human creators now proudly add "No AI" to their video titles. Being made by a person has become a selling point.
▸ A hand-drawn badge with a pencil icon; Pip nodding.

## OUTRO — What It Says About Us (≈7:50)

So why do AI animal memes go viral? Because they hit the oldest buttons we have: cute faces, harmless chaos, catchy rhythms, and stories we tell together, delivered by the newest machine ever built.
▸ ⭐ Callback timeline: cave wall → Egyptian cat → knight vs snail → Brighton cat → LOLcat monitor → phone; Pip walking past each, costume changing.

The machine is new. The monkey brain watching it is very, very old.
▸ Pip and a caveman-Pip sitting side by side, both staring at the same glowing phone.

And yes, we use AI tools on this channel too. The difference is that a human checks every single fact before it reaches you. *(optional line)*
▸ Pip at a desk with a magnifying glass and a pile of books, ticking items off a list.

If you want to know why the internet works the way it does, subscribe. See you next time.
▸ Pip waving; red button shape (no text).
