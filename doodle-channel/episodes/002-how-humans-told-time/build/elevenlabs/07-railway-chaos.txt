Now, even in the 1800s, every town still set its clocks by its own sun. Noon was whenever the sun was highest — right there, locally.

So noon in one city might be several minutes different from noon in the next city over. No big deal — until trains showed up.

Railways needed schedules. And schedules with dozens of different local times were a total mess.

Britain fixed it first. By the late 1840s, most British railways were running on London time.

In North America, on November eighteenth, 1883, the railroads switched to standard time zones. People called it the Day of Two Noons, because many towns saw noon twice as clocks were reset.

A year later, in 1884, delegates from twenty-five countries met in Washington, and chose Greenwich, in London, as the prime meridian: zero degrees longitude.

Time stopped being local. It became global.
