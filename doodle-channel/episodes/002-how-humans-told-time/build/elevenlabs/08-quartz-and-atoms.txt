In 1927, engineers at Bell Labs built the first quartz clock.

Quartz is a crystal with a superpower. Squeeze it, and it makes electricity. Zap it with electricity, and it vibrates — at a very steady rate.

Count those vibrations, and you've got a clock. A typical quartz watch crystal vibrates exactly thirty-two thousand, seven hundred and sixty-eight times per second.

In 1969, Seiko released the Astron, the world's first quartz wristwatch. Within a couple of decades, cheap and accurate quartz watches were everywhere.

But scientists wanted even better. So they looked inside the atom.

In 1955, Louis Essen and Jack Parry in Britain built the first accurate atomic clock, using cesium atoms.

Atoms of the same type vibrate at exactly the same frequency. Always. Everywhere. They're nature's perfect metronome.

In 1967, the world officially redefined the second. One second is now the time it takes for nine billion, one hundred ninety-two million, six hundred thirty-one thousand, seven hundred and seventy cycles of radiation from a cesium atom.

Yep. The second is no longer based on the Earth's spin. It's based on atoms.
