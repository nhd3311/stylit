## COLD OPEN — "Where are we?" (0:00)

October 22nd, 1707. Night. Off the southwest tip of England, a British fleet is sailing home from war.
▸ ⭐ Dark cobalt sea, a line of tall sailing ships under a thin moon; Pip (sailor costume) at the bow, squinting into fog.

The navigators believe they're safely out in open water. They are wrong.
▸ Close-up: an officer's mitten hand pointing at a chart, confident; Pip behind him, unsure.

Within hours, four warships smash into the rocks of the Isles of Scilly. More than thirteen hundred sailors die in a single night. [1]
▸ ⭐ Sharp black rocks, broken masts, vermilion lantern glow; no bodies, a sombre wide shot.

Historians still argue about exactly what went wrong that night. But it became the most famous symbol of a terrifying problem: at sea, nobody really knew where they were. [1][2] 🔧
▸ Pip alone on a tiny raft in a huge empty blue ocean, a giant question mark in the sky made of stars.

And the solution, strangely enough, wasn't a better map. It was a better clock.
▸ A paper map crumples away; a pocket watch drops in its place with a mustard sparkle.

To understand why, we have to go back. Way back. To the very first time a human asked: what time is it?
▸ Pip pulls a huge rewind lever; collage spirals; TITLE CARD.

## CH1 — Chasing Shadows (≈0:47)

For most of human history, nobody needed to know the time. The sun came up, you got up. The sun went down, you slept. Done.
▸ Two panels: caveman-Pip yawning at sunrise / snoring by a fire under the moon.

What really mattered were the seasons. When will the river flood? When do the herds move? When do we plant?
▸ Pip thinking, three cut-paper icons orbiting his head: wave, antelope, sprout.

So people became sky-watchers. At Stonehenge, giant stones line up with the sun on the solstices, the longest and shortest days of the year. [3]
▸ Stonehenge silhouette, mustard sun rising exactly through a gap; tiny Pip pointing.

But to track time within a single day, you needed something much simpler. A stick. Push it into the ground, and its shadow swings around as the sun crosses the sky.
▸ ⭐ A stick in the ground, shadow sweeping across warm paper (timelapse).

The ancient Egyptians turned that stick into real instruments. In 2013, archaeologists in the Valley of the Kings found a small limestone sundial, scratched with hour lines, more than three thousand years old. [4] 🔧
▸ Egyptian-Pip with a tiny brush dusting off a flat stone with lines, cliffs behind.

The Egyptians split daylight into twelve hours, and the night into twelve more. That's where our twenty-four-hour day comes from. [5]
▸ A circle half mustard (sun, 12 ticks) half cobalt (moon, 12 ticks).

But those hours weren't fixed. Daylight was always cut into twelve pieces, so a summer hour was longer than a winter hour. [5]
▸ Two bars, long summer vs short winter, both cut into 12.

Imagine your work shift stretching every summer. No thanks.
▸ Pip melting at a desk under a giant sun, papers piling up.

And sundials have one obvious bug: no sun, no time. Night? Clouds? Good luck.
▸ Pip staring at a sundial under a rain cloud, no shadow.

## CH2 — Water and the Magic Number 60 (≈2:06)

So the Egyptians tried water. A water clock is basically a bowl with a tiny hole near the bottom. Water drips out at a steady rate, and marks on the inside show how much time has passed.
▸ Cross-section of a stone bowl, thin stream dripping, level marks inside.

The oldest one that survives was found broken into pieces at the temple of Karnak. It's decorated with the pharaoh Amenhotep the Third, which dates it to about thirty-four hundred years ago. [6]
▸ Museum-style: the bowl reassembled from cracked pieces, a stylised pharaoh profile on its side.

Water clocks spread everywhere. In ancient Athens, they timed speeches in court. Talk too long? Your water runs out, and so does your argument. [7]
▸ Greek-Pip in toga mid-speech; the judge points at a nearly empty water jar.

Meanwhile, in Mesopotamia, the Babylonians were doing their math in base sixty.
▸ Bearded Babylonian figure pressing wedge marks into a clay tablet; big "60" shape (added in edit).

Sixty is a fantastic number for sharing. You can split it evenly by two, three, four, five, six, ten, twelve, fifteen, twenty and thirty.
▸ A round flatbread cut into many equal slices, several Pips sharing happily.

Greek astronomers borrowed that system, and it stuck. That's why an hour has sixty minutes, and a minute has sixty seconds. Every time you check the clock, you're doing Babylonian math. [5]
▸ Modern Pip checking a wall clock; a ghostly Babylonian gives a thumbs-up behind him.

## CH3 — Gears and Bells (≈3:10)

Fast forward to China, in the 1080s. The emperor orders a scholar named Su Song to build the ultimate astronomical clock. [8]
▸ Su Song (robe, scholar's hat) bowing before a throne; scroll of plans.

By 1094, it's finished: a towering, multi-storey machine in the capital, Kaifeng. [8] 🔧
▸ ⭐ Tall wooden tower, armillary sphere on top, Pip tiny at its base, jaw dropped.

A giant water wheel turns it, one precise click at a time, thanks to a clever device called an escapement, an idea Chinese engineers had been refining since the 700s. [8]
▸ Cutaway: water wheel with buckets, a lever catching and releasing it notch by notch.

It rings bells, beats drums, shows little figures at the right hours, and turns a model of the stars.
▸ Small puppet figures popping out of doors with bells and a drum; star globe rotating.

Then, in 1127, an invading army captured Kaifeng and carted the clock away. It was never successfully rebuilt. [8]
▸ Soldiers with carts hauling gears away; Pip watching sadly.

In Europe, the push for better clocks came from monks. Monasteries prayed at fixed times, day and night, and somebody had to ring the bell on time.
▸ Monk-Pip half-asleep pulling a bell rope, moon in the window.

The English word "clock" even comes from the medieval Latin *clocca*: bell. [9]
▸ A big swinging bell with sound-wave rings.

By the late 1200s, Europe had its first mechanical clocks: heavy weights pulling on ropes, gears turning, and an escapement to slow it all down. [10]
▸ Diagram: falling weight, rope around drum, a few big gears, a swinging bar on top.

They were huge, loud, and honestly pretty bad. The earliest ones may have drifted by an hour or more every single day. [10] 🔧
▸ Pip covering his ears next to a clanking iron clock puffing smoke.

Many didn't even have a minute hand. Why bother?
▸ A clock face with one lonely hand; Pip shrugging.

But towns loved them. Clock towers became the pride of medieval cities, and whole cities started living by the same bell.
▸ Medieval town, central clock tower, sound waves over the rooftops.

## CH4 — The Pendulum (≈4:36)

Now: Italy, the 1580s. According to a famous story, a young student named Galileo is bored in Pisa Cathedral, watching a lamp swing from the ceiling. [11]
▸ Young curly-haired figure on a pew staring up at a swinging lamp; Pip beside him, also staring.

Timing it with his own pulse, he notices something strange: big swings and small swings seem to take the same time.
▸ Two pendulums, wide arc vs small arc, same heartbeat icon above each.

Fun twist: historians later showed that the famous lamp hanging there today was installed after Galileo's student days. So the story might be… slightly decorated. [11] 🔧
▸ Pip as a detective with a magnifying glass, lamp with a "?" tag.

But Galileo really did study pendulums. And in 1656, the Dutch scientist Christiaan Huygens used that idea to build the first pendulum clock. [12]
▸ Huygens (long curly wig) presenting a tall pendulum clock.

The result was massive. The best clocks went from drifting around fifteen minutes a day to about fifteen seconds. [12]
▸ ⭐ Pendulum swinging hypnotically; a vermilion error arrow shrinks from huge to tiny.

For the next two hundred and seventy years, the pendulum ruled timekeeping.
▸ A pendulum clock wearing a crown on a throne.

## CH5 — The Impossible Clock (≈5:27)

Which brings us back to those ships.
▸ Smash cut back to the stormy cold-open sea.

At sea, finding your latitude, how far north or south you are, was doable. Measure the height of the sun at noon, or the North Star at night.
▸ Sailor-Pip with a sighting instrument aimed at the sun; north–south arrow.

But longitude, how far east or west, was a nightmare.
▸ Same Pip turning the instrument east–west; it shows a giant "?" in the sky.

Here's the trick: the Earth turns fifteen degrees every hour. So if you know the time back home and the local time where you are, the difference tells you your longitude.
▸ Globe with meridian lines, two clocks floating above, arrow between them.

Local time is easy: just watch the sun. Home time? You'd need a clock that keeps near-perfect time for months, on a rocking, freezing, salty ship. And a pendulum on a ship? Useless.
▸ Pendulum clock on a tilting deck, swinging wildly with dizzy spiral eyes.

In 1714, the British Parliament offered up to twenty thousand pounds to anyone who could solve it. That's millions in today's money. [13]
▸ Wigged official holding up a huge money bag.

Enter John Harrison: a self-taught carpenter from rural England, who decided to build the impossible clock. [14]
▸ Harrison in a carpenter's apron at a workbench full of wooden gears; Pip as his apprentice.

It took him decades and four attempts. His fourth, the H4, looked like a giant pocket watch. In 1761, his son William carried it on a voyage to Jamaica. [14]
▸ ⭐ The H4 glowing mustard on a ship crossing a collage ocean map, England → Jamaica.

After eighty-one days at sea, it was only about five seconds off. [14]
▸ Pip as captain gasping at the watch; tiny "5s" added in edit.

Did Harrison get his prize? Well… the Board of Longitude kept moving the goalposts. It took years of fighting, and help from King George the Third himself, before Parliament finally paid him in 1773. [14]
▸ Old Harrison arguing with a table of wigged board members; a king figure stepping in.

He died three years later. But his idea changed navigation forever.
▸ Quiet shot: the H4 on a velvet cushion, sunset light.

## CH6 — Railway Chaos (≈7:00)

By the 1800s, clocks were everywhere. But every town still set them by its own sun. Noon was whenever the sun was highest, right there, locally.
▸ Two little towns, each with its own sun overhead, clocks disagreeing.

So noon in Bristol came about ten minutes after noon in London. Nobody cared. Until trains arrived. [15]
▸ A steam train racing between the two towns, vermilion "!" above.

A timetable with a different time at every station is a recipe for missed trains, and worse.
▸ Station-master Pip buried under timetables, walls full of clocks showing different times.

In 1840, the Great Western Railway put all its stations on London time. By the late 1840s, most of Britain's railways had followed. In Bristol, the old Corn Exchange clock still has two minute hands: one for London, one for Bristol. [15] 🔧
▸ ⭐ The Bristol Corn Exchange clock face with TWO minute hands; Pip squinting at it.

In North America, the chaos was even bigger: dozens of different railroad times. So on November 18th, 1883, the railroads switched to standard time zones. [16]
▸ Map of North America split into cut-paper stripes.

Newspapers called it the Day of Two Noons, because in many towns the clock struck twelve, was set back, and then struck twelve again. [16]
▸ Townsfolk-Pip looking at a clock tower with two suns in the sky.

A year later, delegates from twenty-five countries met in Washington and voted to make Greenwich, in London, the prime meridian: zero degrees longitude. [17]
▸ Round table of delegates; a globe with one vermilion line.

Not everyone was happy. France abstained and kept using Paris time on its own maps for decades. [17] 🔧
▸ A French delegate with folded arms, a tiny Eiffel-free Paris skyline (keep it generic), Pip giggling.

But time had stopped being local. It had become global.
▸ Globe wrapped in neat time-zone stripes, clocks orbiting it.

## CH7 — Quartz and Atoms (≈8:20)

In 1927, engineers at Bell Labs built the first quartz clock. [18]
▸ Two engineers beside a big cabinet with wires and a glowing crystal.

Quartz has a superpower. Squeeze it, and it makes a tiny electric voltage. Zap it with electricity, and it vibrates, at an incredibly steady rate.
▸ A quartz crystal with a little cape, lightning bolts and vibration lines.

Count those vibrations, and you've got a clock. The crystal in a typical quartz watch vibrates exactly thirty-two thousand, seven hundred and sixty-eight times a second.
▸ Opened wristwatch, tiny tuning-fork crystal buzzing.

On Christmas Day 1969, Seiko released the Astron in Tokyo, the world's first quartz wristwatch. It cost four hundred and fifty thousand yen, about the price of a small car. [19] 🔧
▸ Pip in a 60s suit admiring a gold watch in a shop window next to a small car.

Within a couple of decades, cheap quartz watches were everywhere.
▸ Crowd of Pips all showing off wristwatches.

But scientists wanted more. So they looked inside the atom.
▸ Scientist-Pip with a giant magnifying glass over an atom.

In 1955, Louis Essen and Jack Parry at Britain's National Physical Laboratory switched on the first practical cesium atomic clock. It was accurate to about one second in three hundred years. [20]
▸ ⭐ Two scientists beside a long metal tube machine with dials, atom symbol pulsing.

Atoms of the same type always vibrate at exactly the same frequency. They're nature's perfect metronome.
▸ A row of identical atoms with identical wavy lines; a metronome at the end.

So in 1967, the world redefined the second itself. One second is now exactly nine billion, one hundred ninety-two million, six hundred thirty-one thousand, seven hundred and seventy cycles of radiation from a cesium atom. [21]
▸ A counter overflowing with digits (added in edit); tiny stopwatch reading 1.

Read that again. The second is no longer based on the Earth spinning. It's based on atoms.
▸ Pip crossing out a spinning Earth with a vermilion X, pointing at a glowing atom.

## CH8 — Time Today (≈9:44)

And you use atomic clocks every single day. Every GPS satellite carries them. [22]
▸ GPS satellite over a cobalt Earth, small atom glowing inside.

Your phone finds you by measuring how long signals take to arrive from several satellites. Light travels about thirty centimetres in a billionth of a second, so an error of just one millionth of a second could put you around three hundred metres off.
▸ Map: Pip's real position vs a pin in the middle of a lake.

Today's best clocks are optical clocks, using atoms like strontium trapped in laser light. They're so precise they wouldn't drift by a second in billions of years, longer than the universe has existed. [23]
▸ ⭐ Glowing cloud of atoms in a lattice of laser beams.

And here's the twist ending. The Earth itself turned out to be a sloppy clock: its spin speeds up and slows down by tiny amounts.
▸ Earth wobbling, sweating, a stopwatch disagreeing with it.

So since 1972, scientists have added twenty-seven "leap seconds" to keep atomic time in step with the planet. [24]
▸ Scientist sliding tiny blocks into a timeline.

But leap seconds break computers. So in October 2026, the world's measurement experts voted to stop adding them, starting May 2027. [25] 🔧 *(new, very recent)*
▸ A smoking computer with dizzy eyes; a calendar page stamped with a vermilion X.

After thousands of years of following the sun, humanity finally made it official: the atoms win.
▸ Sun and atom on a boxing stage; referee-Pip raising the atom's hand.

So, from a stick in the ground, to a bowl of water, a tower of gears, a swinging pendulum, a watch on a ship, a crystal on your wrist and atoms in space…
▸ ⭐ Long timeline: Pip walks past every invention, his costume changing at each one.

humans spent thousands of years chasing one simple question: what time is it?
▸ Pip under a night sky, the "?" curl glowing.

And the funny part? Now that we can measure time almost perfectly… we still never have enough of it.
▸ Pip sprinting late with a coffee, giant clock behind, papers flying.
